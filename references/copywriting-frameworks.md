# Copywriting Frameworks

Research note for any client. Generic on purpose. Client voice, offer, and facts live in that client's `knowledge/`. This file is the craft: how strong copy is built.

Read this when writing or reviewing ads, landing pages, emails, captions, or site copy. Then apply the client's `voice-guidelines.md`. Voice wins on wording. These frameworks win on whether a sentence earns its place.

This is not a 1610 house style guide. It is a map of the classic systems, named to their authors, with a way to use each one.

---

## How to use this file

Pick one primary framework for the piece. Do not stack three headlines. A landing page is usually Schwartz (who is this person) + Hopkins or Ogilvy (what is true and specific) + one close (PAS, AIDA, or a direct ask). An ad that already has search intent can skip the problem and start further down.

Test: after you write, name which framework each section is serving. If you cannot, cut the section.

## 1. Eugene Schwartz — stages of awareness

From *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966). The headline is decided by what the reader already knows, not by what you want to say.

| Stage | They know | Copy starts with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Nothing. Not even a feeling. | A story or curiosity that creates the problem |
| Problem Aware | Something is wrong. No named fix. | The problem in their words |
| Solution Aware | A kind of fix exists. Not your product. | The mechanism, compared to what they already tried |
| Product Aware | You exist. Not sold yet. | Proof, difference, offer |
| Most Aware | Ready. Needs the ask. | Price, terms, CTA |

**How to run it**

1. Name the stage of the person who will see this piece. One stage.
2. Write the first screen only for that stage.
3. Do not lead with product language (features, process names, dashboards, "our system") if they are still Problem Aware.
4. Ads that match a typed query are often Solution or Product Aware. Cold social and a homepage are usually Problem Aware.

**Fails when:** you write Product-Aware copy for a Problem-Aware reader. That is the most common miss.

## 2. Claude Hopkins — scientific advertising

From *Scientific Advertising* (1923) and the Schlitz / Palmolive / Pepsodent work. Advertising is salesmanship in print. Specifics sell. Generalities do not.

**How to run it**

- One offer, one reader, one action.
- Prefer a reason and a number over an adjective. "We wash every bottle with live steam" beat "pure beer."
- Sell service, not the firm. The reader cares what they get.
- Put a specific claim in the headline when you have one that is true every time.
- Test. Hopkins treated copy as experiments, not art.

**Fails when:** the line could sit on a competitor's page unchanged. "Quality you can trust" is not a claim.

---

## 3. John Caples — tested headlines

From *Tested Advertising Methods* (1932) and the "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano" ad. He split-tested headlines for decades. Most of the lift was in the first line.

Caples' useful types (use one):

1. Self-interest — what they get
2. News — what changed
3. Curiosity — only if the body pays it off immediately
4. How-to / implied how-to
5. Question — only if they would actually ask it
6. Command — rare; feels like an order

**How to run it**

- Write 10 headlines. Ship the most specific self-interest or news line, not the cleverest.
- Long headlines are fine if every word works.
- Specificity beats elegance. "How I improved my memory in one evening" outran vague prestige lines.

**Fails when:** the headline is a metaphor that does not name the section or the benefit.

---

## 4. David Ogilvy — research, then one big idea

From *Ogilvy on Advertising* and the Hathaway / Rolls-Royce / Dove work. "The consumer is not a moron. She is your wife." Research first. Then one idea, said plainly, with facts.

**How to run it**

- Do the homework before the headline. Who buys, why, what they already believe.
- One big idea per piece. Supporting facts, not a second idea.
- Long copy works when the reader is interested. Do not shorten past the proof.
- Specifics: "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
- Never write an ad you would not want your own family to believe.

**Fails when:** you decorate instead of inform, or you pack three ideas into one hero.

---

## 5. Rosser Reeves — USP

From *Reality in Advertising* (1961). Unique Selling Proposition. Not a slogan. A claim the competition cannot or does not make, that the reader actually wants.

Three tests, all required:

1. **Buy this and you get this specific benefit.**
2. **The competition cannot or does not say it.**
3. **It is strong enough to move people.**

**How to run it**

- Write the benefit in a sentence a stranger could repeat.
- Kill lines that any competitor could run tomorrow.
- Repeat the USP. Reeves believed confusion, not boredom, was the enemy.

**Fails when:** the "unique" line is a vibe ("we care more") or a process name the buyer did not ask for.

---

## 6. Robert Collier — enter the conversation already happening

From *The Robert Collier Letter Book*. The letter works when it starts where the reader's head already is, not where you wish it were.

**How to run it**

- Open with their situation, their last action, or the thing they said out loud this week.
- Then build a bridge to the offer. Do not start with you.
- Picture the one person. Write to them.

**Fails when:** the first line is the firm's category or a mission statement.

---

## 7. Gary Halbert — one reader, a starvng crowd, simple English

From *The Boron Letters* and the newsletter. Find a market that already wants the thing. Write like a letter to one person. Simple words.

**How to run it**

- Prefer a hungry market over clever copy.
- Short words. Short sentences. One person, "you."
- AIDA as a letter, not a template: attention, then interest they feel, then proof, then a clear ask.
- If a 12-year-old cannot follow it, rewrite.

**Fails when:** you write to "businesses," "organizations," or "leaders."

---

## 8. Eugene Schwartz / market sophistication (pair with awareness)

Also *Breakthrough Advertising*. After awareness: how tired is this *kind* of claim in the market?

| Stage | Market has heard | You need |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nothing like this | The claim, said straight |
| 2 | The claim | A bigger or newer claim |
| 3 | Many claims | Mechanism: *how* it works |
| 4 | Many mechanisms | A new mechanism |
| 5 | Everything | Identification, story, or a smaller true claim |

**How to run it:** if the category is noisy, do not shout a louder promise. Change the mechanism or get more specific. If the category is new, say the benefit plainly.

## 9. PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve

Direct-response workhorse (often credited through later mail and web copy; the pattern is older). Good for Problem-Aware readers.

1. **Problem** — name it in their words
2. **Agitate** — what it costs them if it stays
3. **Solve** — the offer, then proof

**How to run it:** one problem. Agitation is specific (time, money, a decision they are avoiding), not drama. Solve must be something you can do every time.

**Fails when:** you agitate three problems or the solve is a metaphor.

---

## 10. AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

Classic sales letter / ad structure (Elmo Lewis, early 1900s; used ever since).

1. **Attention** — headline
2. **Interest** — why this matters to them
3. **Desire** — proof, picture of the result, offer
4. **Action** — one ask

**How to run it:** do not repeat Attention in Interest. Each step must add. Action is a verb plus what happens next.

---

## 11. Features → Advantages → Benefits (and "so what")

Hopkins / later B2B training. A feature is a fact about the thing. A benefit is what changes for the reader.

**How to run it**

- Write the feature.
- Ask "so what?" until a human outcome appears.
- Ship the outcome. Keep the feature as proof, not as the headline.

Example shape: "Closes the books by the fifth" (feature) → "you know cash before payroll" (benefit).

---

## 12. Proof next to the ask

Hopkins, Ogilvy, Reeves, Caples all treated proof as part of the sale, not a decoration band.

**How to run it**

- Specific and checkable beats big and round.
- One real case with a real number beats a stack of adjectives.
- Put proof next to the decision (near the CTA), not in an orphan metrics row.
- If you cannot defend the number on a bad week, do not print it.

---

## 13. One piece, one action

Scientific advertising again. Also every decent landing-page test since.

**How to run it**

- One primary CTA. Name the action in the button.
- Secondary links are allowed if they do not compete (legal, a quiet alternative).
- If the page needs two asks, it is two pages.

## A short working order

When you sit down to write:

1. **Schwartz:** what stage is this reader?
2. **Collier / Halbert:** what conversation are they already in?
3. **Reeves:** what can we say that a competitor cannot?
4. **Hopkins / Ogilvy:** what specific, always-true fact proves it?
5. **Caples:** write 10 first lines; pick the plainest that still names the benefit or the problem.
6. **PAS or AIDA:** structure the body.
7. **One action.** Proof beside it.

Then run the client's voice guide and the humanizer. Frameworks choose the bones. Voice chooses the words.
